# Draft research evidence note: suicidal thoughts or self-harm with crisis-support pathway

> **DRAFT RESEARCH ONLY — NOT USER-FACING ADVICE.** This note is not clinical approval, a risk-assessment instrument, a safety plan, or a substitute for local emergency/mental-health services. It must not be used to prescribe a unified LifeSafe app protocol. Any product wording, routing, escalation, safeguarding, or clinical claims require focused clinician, safeguarding, legal, and jurisdiction review.
>
> **Access date for all sources:** 2026-11-09. Source pages were opened and read directly. This note records public-source evidence available on that date; it does not establish that any contact detail or service operation remains current after that date.

## Scope

This note covers public-source evidence about responses when a person reports suicidal thoughts, may be about to self-harm, has self-harmed, or when another person is concerned about them. The focus is the existence and shape of a crisis-support pathway: recognizing possible urgency, connecting with emergency or crisis services, and involving trusted people or professionals. It does not define clinical risk thresholds, diagnose intent, specify a safety-plan algorithm, recommend means-restriction implementation, or select a country-independent helpline.

The terms *suicidal thoughts*, *self-harm*, *suicide attempt*, and *mental-health crisis* are not interchangeable. The reviewed pages use overlapping but non-identical categories. In particular, self-harm can occur without stated suicidal intent, while some people who self-harm may have elevated suicide risk. That distinction needs clinician review before any user-facing language or triage logic is written. [1] [3]

## Source-supported findings

- WHO describes suicide as occurring across the life course and in all regions, and reports that suicide attempts are much more frequent than deaths. It identifies suicidal behaviour as influenced by multiple factors and recommends early identification, assessment, management, and follow-up within a broader, multisectoral national prevention response. WHO also notes that people may act impulsively during a crisis and that stigma can reduce help-seeking. These are global public-health findings, not a validated individual prediction rule. [1]

- The NHS suicidal-thoughts page says that a person who feels they want to end their life should tell someone and that support is available. It separates ordinary support options from an emergency route: if a person has seriously harmed themselves, such as through an overdose, or feels they may be about to harm themselves, it directs them to call 999 for an ambulance or go to A&E. It also lists NHS 111, a GP emergency appointment, a mental-health crisis team where one exists, and named UK helplines/text services. The page says that someone worried about another person may call the listed helplines and emphasizes listening and taking the disclosure seriously. [2]

- The NHS self-harm page says support is available for people who self-harm or think about self-harm and for friends and family. It advises speaking to a GP for self-harm, thoughts of self-harm, or concern about minor injuries, while directing people needing help now for a mental-health crisis or emergency to the NHS urgent-help route. It states that some people who self-harm are at higher risk of suicide, but it does not provide a universal numeric threshold or imply that every episode has the same intent or urgency. [3]

- Australian healthdirect similarly separates immediate danger from other crisis support. Its page states that if a person with suicidal thoughts is in immediate danger, the Australian emergency number is triple zero (000); it also identifies hospitals, Lifeline, Beyond Blue, Suicide Call Back Service, and state/territory services. For a loved one in crisis, it points to an emergency department, emergency services when the person is a danger to self or others, and local crisis assessment services. The page says professionals assess needs and decide the best care; possible care can include hospital or home support. [4]

- The US 988 Lifeline page is directed at people supporting someone else. It recommends direct, open, matter-of-fact conversation about suicide, listening without judgment, not promising secrecy when a person discloses a plan, seeking crisis-intervention help, and following up. It presents the 988 service and associated action steps as a US pathway. It does not establish that these steps, service numbers, confidentiality rules, or emergency routes apply outside the US. [5]

Taken together, the reviewed sources support a general evidence statement rather than one universal protocol: a credible crisis pathway should distinguish possible immediate danger from non-immediate support needs; offer a route to locally appropriate emergency services; make it possible to connect with trained crisis or health professionals; and support involving a trusted person when appropriate. The sources do not support embedding one global emergency number, one fixed risk score, or one automatic disposition in every jurisdiction. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

## Population and jurisdiction limits

WHO's material is global and population-level. It should not be read as a country-specific service directory or as evidence that a particular intervention is available, effective, or legally appropriate for every population. [1]

The NHS pages are for the United Kingdom's NHS context, with UK-specific numbers, services, and terms such as 999, NHS 111, A&E, GP, and community mental-health team. Their service descriptions and contact details must not be transplanted to another country. The self-harm page was last reviewed 23 August 2023 and the suicidal-thoughts page was last reviewed 26 April 2024; these review dates are part of the source context and do not independently verify present-day availability. [2] [3]

Healthdirect is Australian and uses Australian emergency and service pathways, including triple zero (000), state and territory services, and Australian hospitals. Its discussion of possible hospital or home support and involuntary hospitalisation is jurisdiction-specific and must not be generalized to another legal system. [4]

988 Lifeline is a US service. Its availability, routing, confidentiality language, and advice to contact 988 are not evidence for non-US jurisdictions. [5]

The reviewed sources do not establish accessibility, language availability, relay/TTY access, rural coverage, age-specific eligibility, immigration or confidentiality implications, or service quality in every location. They also do not establish a single pathway for children, adolescents, older adults, disabled people, people in institutions, people intoxicated or medically injured, or people who cannot safely speak on a phone. These gaps require local review rather than inference.

## Disagreements and uncertainties

The pages differ in how they describe urgency. NHS uses examples such as serious self-harm/overdose or feeling about to self-harm; healthdirect uses immediate danger and danger to self or others; 988 focuses on disclosure of a plan and contacting crisis support. These are not equivalent definitions and should not be combined into a single app threshold without clinical validation. [2] [4] [5]

The pages also differ in pathway design. The UK page foregrounds 999, A&E, NHS 111, GP, crisis teams, and named listening services; Australia foregrounds 000, emergency departments, national/state services, and crisis assessment teams; the US page foregrounds 988 and local 988 centers. This is a service-system difference, not evidence that one route is universally preferable. [2] [4] [5]

The reviewed public pages do not provide enough evidence to determine whether a digital self-assessment, chatbot, automated risk classifier, or app-generated safety plan can safely replace clinical assessment or emergency dispatch. They do not establish sensitivity, specificity, false-negative risk, or outcome benefit for such tools. No credible evidence for a LifeSafe-specific protocol was verified in this review.

## What must not be inferred

- Do not infer that absence of stated suicidal intent means absence of danger, or that every self-harm episode is a suicide attempt.
- Do not infer that a user who denies immediate danger is safe, that a user who cannot answer is low risk, or that a fixed question set can determine disposition without clinical validation.
- Do not infer that a helpline can provide emergency medical care, ambulance dispatch, wound treatment, safeguarding, or continuity of care in every jurisdiction.
- Do not copy 999, 000, 988, NHS 111, A&E, or any named service into a global product flow without location verification and current service-owner confirmation.
- Do not infer that the reviewed sources approve automated escalation, contacting a third party, removing means, mandatory reporting, involuntary treatment, or disclosure of user data. Those actions depend on local law, policy, consent, immediacy, and professional judgment.
- Do not infer that advice for adults applies unchanged to children or adolescents, or that advice for a person seeking help applies unchanged to a bystander.
- Do not infer that “confidential” means absolute secrecy, or that a service's information-sharing practice is the same across services or countries.
- Do not infer clinical efficacy, regulatory approval, or safety from the existence of a public information page.

## Focused clinician review questions

1. What clinically meaningful distinctions must the product preserve between suicidal thoughts, suicidal intent, a plan, access to means, recent self-harm, suicide attempt, medically serious injury, and concern about another person?
2. Which signs require emergency medical advice or emergency dispatch, and how should uncertainty, inability to answer, intoxication, communication barriers, and third-party reports be handled?
3. What population-specific safeguards are needed for children and adolescents, pregnancy/postpartum, older adults, people with cognitive or communication disabilities, and people in unsafe or coercive environments?
4. What information can be offered before location is known, and what minimum location/service verification is required before displaying emergency numbers or local crisis contacts?
5. When is it clinically and legally appropriate to suggest involving a trusted person, and what should the product say when involving that person could increase danger?
6. What claims about listening, means safety, safety planning, follow-up, or crisis-line confidentiality are supported by current evidence and local policy?
7. What human handoff, safeguarding, documentation, audit, and failure-recovery process is required if the product detects possible imminent danger?
8. What clinical outcome measures, adverse-event monitoring, equity checks, and independent validation would be required before any triage or automated recommendation is considered?

## Language-review terms

Review for plain, non-stigmatizing, non-coercive wording around: *suicidal thoughts*; *thinking about suicide*; *self-harm*; *suicide attempt*; *immediate danger*; *about to harm yourself*; *serious injury*; *overdose*; *emergency services*; *crisis support*; *mental-health crisis*; *trusted person*; *friend or family member*; *listen without judgment*; *do not keep a plan secret*; *confidentiality and its limits*; *children and young people*; *local services*; and *call, text, or chat*. Review whether “commit suicide,” “attention-seeking,” “manipulative,” “successful/failed attempt,” “just self-harm,” and other potentially stigmatizing or misleading terms should be excluded. A language review must preserve distinctions among thoughts, intent, plans, acts, injury, and medical urgency rather than collapsing them into a single label.
