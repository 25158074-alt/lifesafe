# LifeSafe evidence note 22: Fire or smoke exposure

> **Status:** DRAFT_UNVERIFIED. Credible public evidence was verified on the pages listed below, but this note is not a completed evidence review and has not been clinically approved.

## Scope

This topic covers health effects and publicly stated safety/emergency considerations for exposure to smoke from wildfires, structural fires, and combustion sources, including possible carbon monoxide exposure. It is deliberately not a complete fire-escape, burn-treatment, toxicology, or respiratory-emergency guideline. The sources mix population-level wildfire-smoke evidence with public-facing emergency advice; they should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Source-supported findings

- WHO describes wildfire smoke as a complex mixture that can include particulate matter, gases, hazardous air pollutants, and other contaminants. Smoke composition and health effects vary with fuel and whether manufactured structures burn. WHO reports short-term associations including headache, eye and respiratory irritation, dizziness, wheezing, chest pain, asthma attacks, shortness of breath, cardiovascular effects, and acute mortality in affected regions. [1]
- WHO identifies children, older adults, pregnant people, people with existing lung or heart disease, people with socioeconomic or housing constraints, outdoor workers, people near fires, and wildland firefighters as groups that may have increased vulnerability or exposure. [1]
- CDC states that wildfire smoke can affect anyone and can travel far from the fire. Its listed immediate effects include cough, trouble breathing, wheezing, asthma attacks, stinging eyes, scratchy throat, runny nose, irritated sinuses, headache, tiredness, chest pain, and fast heartbeat. CDC lists people with chronic conditions, pregnant people, children, and responders as higher-risk groups. [2]
- EPA states that health effects depend on both the amount and duration of exposure. Its short-term list includes respiratory symptoms, reduced lung function, asthma and other lung-disease exacerbation, cardiovascular events and care utilization, preterm birth, and premature death. EPA also states that most available knowledge concerns exposures over a day to a few days, while less is known about longer, repeated, episodic, and cumulative exposures. [4]
- WHO and EPA both support exposure-reduction as a public-health consideration during smoke events, including consulting relevant/local authorities, reducing outdoor activity, seeking cleaner indoor air, and using local air-quality information. CDC’s U.S. page additionally discusses indoor filtration, respirators when going outside, and following local emergency-management instructions. These are source-described public-health measures, not a proposed LifeSafe workflow. [1] [3] [4]
- NHS states that carbon monoxide can be produced by fires and fuel-burning appliances, is colourless and odourless, and can cause headache, dizziness, nausea/vomiting, weakness, tiredness, confusion, chest or muscle pain, shortness of breath, flushing, and loss of vision. The NHS page directs people who suspect exposure to leave the affected building and obtain medical advice; it identifies breathing difficulty, sudden confusion, loss of consciousness, weakness, or chest/muscle pain as reasons for emergency help in its UK system. [5]
- NHS poisoning guidance states that breathing in a harmful substance can be poisoning and frames severe breathing difficulty, loss of consciousness, stopped breathing, or seizure as emergencies in the UK system. It also cautions that symptoms can take hours or days to start, so absence of immediate symptoms does not establish safety. [6]

## Population and jurisdiction limits

WHO’s page is global and population-level, with broad wildfire-smoke framing; it does not establish a patient-specific threshold or a universal emergency decision rule. CDC and EPA pages are U.S.-government public guidance, use U.S. systems and terminology such as AirNow, AQI, NIOSH approval, and U.S. emergency-management instructions, and should not be assumed to map directly to another country’s air-quality index, respirator availability, emergency number, or clinical pathway. NHS pages are public guidance for the UK NHS context and use 999, A&E, and NHS 111; they should not be copied as location-neutral contact instructions.

The evidence is strongest for short-term wildfire-smoke exposure and may not generalize to every structural-fire smoke, industrial fire, indoor combustion, or chemical-release scenario. Risks differ by smoke composition, ventilation, dose, duration, heat, enclosed-space exposure, age, pregnancy, chronic disease, disability, medications, and ability to evacuate or obtain cleaner air. The cited public pages do not provide a validated LifeSafe-specific risk model.

## Disagreements and uncertainties

The sources are broadly aligned that smoke can irritate the eyes and respiratory tract and worsen cardiopulmonary disease, but they differ in emphasis and granularity. WHO presents global health impacts and notes that evidence for cardiovascular and respiratory mortality is mixed and less conclusive, while EPA lists cardiovascular outcomes associated with short-term wildfire-smoke exposure and explicitly distinguishes the better-studied short-term period from less-known longer exposures. This is not necessarily a contradiction; it reflects different outcome framing and evidence boundaries.

Long-term and cumulative effects remain incompletely characterized. WHO says long-term effects are under investigation and EPA says less is known about repeated or cumulative exposure. The pages do not establish a single symptom threshold, safe exposure duration, universal indoor-air target, or universal respirator rule for all people and all fire types.

Carbon monoxide is a distinct hazard that may accompany fire or combustion exposure and cannot be ruled out by smell, but these public pages do not provide a complete diagnostic or treatment algorithm. Symptoms may overlap with smoke irritation, heat illness, anxiety, or other medical emergencies. Delayed symptoms and changing exposure conditions are material uncertainties.

## What must not be inferred

- Do not infer that a person is safe because smoke is not visible, has no smell, or symptoms are absent initially.
- Do not infer that wildfire-smoke advice covers structural fires, enclosed-space fires, industrial/chemical fires, or all combustion exposures.
- Do not infer a universal AQI number, PM2.5 threshold, exposure duration, distance-from-fire rule, respirator fit rule, or emergency-call trigger from these sources.
- Do not infer that any one symptom, symptom combination, or self-reported exposure can diagnose smoke inhalation, carbon monoxide poisoning, airway injury, or another condition.
- Do not infer that a face covering, indoor filtration, or staying indoors is protective in every fire, evacuation, oxygen-deficient, or toxic-gas situation.
- Do not infer that U.S. AQI/AirNow/NIOSH language or UK 999/111/A&E language is globally applicable.
- Do not infer that the listed higher-risk groups are exhaustive or that people outside them have low risk.
- Do not infer that this note approves a LifeSafe alert, escalation, triage, treatment, or evacuation protocol.

## Focused clinician review questions

1. Which fire/smoke scenarios are in scope for LifeSafe: wildfire smoke, building fire, indoor combustion, carbon monoxide concern, chemical fire, or only some of these?
2. Which symptom and exposure features should trigger immediate emergency escalation, urgent clinician contact, or non-urgent information, and how should local emergency numbers and services be configured by jurisdiction?
3. How should delayed or worsening symptoms be represented without implying that an initial symptom-free state is safe?
4. What additional safeguards are needed for children, pregnancy, older adults, cardiopulmonary disease, disability, cognitive impairment, language barriers, and people unable to evacuate or access cleaner air?
5. How should possible carbon monoxide exposure be separated from general smoke irritation, and what wording avoids diagnosis while still avoiding false reassurance?
6. What must the app say about scene safety, evacuation orders, re-entry, and not delaying professional or emergency help?
7. If air-quality data are shown, which national/local data source, units, update cadence, uncertainty statement, and stale-data behavior are clinically and legally acceptable?
8. If respirators or indoor filtration are mentioned, what fit, age, contraindication, availability, and failure-mode caveats are required for the target jurisdictions?
9. What evidence and governance are required before any symptom checker, notification, or escalation rule can be approved for release?

## Language-review terms

Review the translations and local usage of: **smoke exposure**, **smoke inhalation**, **wildfire smoke**, **fire smoke**, **fumes**, **carbon monoxide**, **poisoning**, **shortness of breath**, **trouble breathing**, **wheezing**, **chest tightness**, **chest pain**, **confusion**, **loss of consciousness**, **airway**, **respirator**, **mask**, **cleaner air**, **evacuate**, **shelter indoors**, **re-entry**, **emergency services**, **urgent medical help**, **children**, **pregnant**, **older adults**, **chronic lung disease**, **heart disease**, and **local air-quality index**. Check that translations distinguish a respirator from a loose face covering, smoke from carbon monoxide, and urgent from emergency care. Avoid wording that promises safety, diagnoses a condition, or implies that one contact number or air-quality scale applies everywhere.
