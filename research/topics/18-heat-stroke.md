# Heat stroke and heat illness — draft evidence note

> **DRAFT RESEARCH ONLY — NOT USER-FACING ADVICE. NOT CLINICAL APPROVAL.**
>
> This note summarizes public sources for clinician and product review. It does not establish a unified LifeSafe protocol, diagnostic rule, triage algorithm, treatment instruction, emergency number, or regulatory/clinical claim.
>
> **Research status:** DRAFT_UNVERIFIED. Sources were opened and read on 2026-11-09. Currentness, clinical applicability, translation, accessibility, and local implementation still require clinician and jurisdictional review.

## Scope

This topic covers heat illness, with emphasis on heat exhaustion and heatstroke, including recognition, urgency, cooling, hydration context, risk groups, and boundaries between public first-aid information and clinical care. It also notes related heat illnesses where the reviewed sources include them (heat cramps, heat syncope, heat rash, and exertional rhabdomyolysis). It does not attempt to review heat illness in pregnancy, infants, medication-specific risk, toxicological hyperthermia, chronic acclimatization programs, or hospital treatment in detail.

## Source-supported findings

- Heatstroke is consistently described as the most severe or life-threatening heat illness and a medical emergency. The CDC/NIOSH describes loss of temperature control, possible rapid temperature rise, central neurological changes, seizures or coma, and risk of permanent disability or death if treatment is delayed. WHO likewise describes heatstroke as a medical emergency with high case fatality. [1] [3]
- Neurological or behavioral change is a recurring warning feature. CDC/NIOSH lists confusion, altered mental status, slurred speech, loss of consciousness, and seizures. NHS lists confusion, restlessness, poor coordination, seizures, and loss of consciousness. St John Ambulance lists restlessness, confusion, unusual behavior, and rapid deterioration in responsiveness. [1] [2] [6]
- The reviewed first-aid and resuscitation sources support getting emergency help for suspected heatstroke while beginning cooling rather than waiting for transport. CDC/NIOSH says to call 911, stay with the person, move them to a cool area, remove outer clothing, and cool quickly. NHS says to call 999 for heatstroke and cool while waiting. The American Red Cross says to call 911 and begin rapid cooling while waiting for EMS. Resuscitation Council UK says that for heatstroke, active cooling methods with the fastest cooling rate should be prioritized. [1] [2] [5] [7]
- Cooling methods named across sources include cold-water or ice immersion where feasible, wetting or sponging the skin, cold/wet sheets or cloths, cold packs, fanning or air circulation, and combinations of these. The Red Cross identifies cold-water immersion up to the neck as its preferred method in its first-aid page; CDC/NIOSH, NHS, St John Ambulance, and Resuscitation Council UK describe alternative or complementary methods. [1] [2] [5] [6] [7]
- Heat exhaustion is presented as less severe than heatstroke but potentially progressing or requiring escalation. Commonly listed symptoms include tiredness or weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea or vomiting, heavy sweating, thirst, cramps, irritability, and elevated temperature. CDC/NIOSH frames heat exhaustion in a worker context and advises medical evaluation; NHS says symptoms should improve after cooling and fluids but directs people to urgent help if they are difficult to treat or persist. [1] [2]
- Heat illness is not limited to outdoor work or a single environmental temperature. CDC/NIOSH includes indoor and outdoor workers. WHO describes environmental heat stress as a combination of temperature, humidity, wind, radiation, clothing, and metabolic heat, and notes risks for indoor workers, manual workers, athletes, older people, people with chronic conditions, pregnant people, migrants, and people with lower acclimatization or poor housing/cooling access. [1] [3] [4]
- WHO describes heat as worsening risks associated with cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, metabolic, and mental-health conditions, and notes that the scale of impact depends on the timing, intensity, and duration of heat, acclimatization, and local infrastructure and institutions. These are population-health findings, not a basis for an individual diagnosis. [3] [4]
- Related conditions have distinct descriptions. CDC/NIOSH includes heat syncope, heat cramps, heat rash, and exertional rhabdomyolysis in its heat-related illness set. Rhabdomyolysis is associated with muscle pain/cramps, weakness, exercise intolerance, or dark urine and requires medical assessment; this should not be treated as interchangeable with heat exhaustion or heatstroke. [1]

## Population and jurisdiction limits

The CDC/NIOSH page is occupational guidance for workers and supervisors in the United States; its emergency number is 911 and its recommendations are not automatically applicable to the general public or another country. [1] The NHS page is public-facing guidance for England/UK services and uses 999 and NHS 111; its 30-minute wording and service routes should not be transplanted to other jurisdictions. [2]

WHO’s pages are global public-health and occupational-health materials. They describe population risk and policy context, not a locally validated individual triage tool. [3] [4] The American Red Cross page is US first-aid education and uses 911/EMS terminology. [5] St John Ambulance is UK first-aid education, clinically reviewed 28 April 2025, and uses 999/112. [6] Resuscitation Council UK’s 2025 guidance is professional resuscitation guidance and is not a substitute for local clinical protocols, ambulance-service instructions, or clinician judgment. [7]

Age, pregnancy, disability, body size, chronic disease, medication use, intoxication, water access, language, cultural practice, available cooling equipment, and local emergency access may alter risk or feasibility. The reviewed pages do not establish a single validated approach for all of these groups.

## Disagreements and uncertainties

- **Temperature threshold:** Sources do not use one universal threshold. CDC/NIOSH mentions a body temperature that can rise to 106°F or higher within 10–15 minutes; St John Ambulance lists above 40°C as a sign; NHS uses “very high temperature” without making a numeric threshold central; WHO and Resuscitation Council UK emphasize severe hyperthermia and neurological dysfunction. A measured threshold must not be required before taking emergency action, and a normal, unavailable, or inaccurate measurement must not be treated as exclusionary. [1] [2] [4] [6] [7]
- **Skin and sweating:** CDC/NIOSH lists hot, dry skin or profuse sweating; NHS lists hot skin without sweating; Red Cross includes moist, pale, or flushed skin and no or some sweating. These differences show that dry skin is not a safe stand-alone requirement and that sweating status is variable. [1] [2] [5]
- **Oral fluids:** Guidance is not uniform across illness severity and source context. NHS and CDC/NIOSH describe fluids for heat exhaustion when the person can take them; the reviewed heatstroke pages prioritize emergency help and cooling and do not establish a universal oral-fluid rule. Resuscitation Council UK mentions provision of fluids for heat syncope/heat exhaustion but not as a substitute for rapid active cooling in heatstroke. Any app wording about drinking, swallowing ability, vomiting, altered consciousness, or aspiration requires focused clinician review. [1] [2] [5] [7]
- **Cooling duration and targets:** Red Cross specifies example durations (15 minutes of immersion or 20 minutes with alternatives, or until improvement/EMS), whereas other sources use “cool quickly,” “while waiting,” or cooling until the condition improves. These are not interchangeable universal targets. [1] [2] [5] [6]
- **Terminology and classification:** “Heat exhaustion,” “heatstroke,” exertional heat illness, environmental heat illness, fever, and toxin-induced hyperthermia can overlap in lay descriptions but are not equivalent clinical diagnoses. Resuscitation Council UK’s hyperthermia section separates heat illness from malignant or toxin-induced hyperthermia. [7]

## What must not be inferred

- Do not infer that a symptom checklist can diagnose or exclude heatstroke, or that absence of sweating, a numeric temperature, or a particular skin appearance rules it out.
- Do not infer that heat exhaustion is always benign, that it will resolve within a fixed period for every person, or that improvement eliminates the need for assessment in a high-risk or uncertain case.
- Do not infer a universal emergency number, response time, cooling duration, temperature cut-off, fluid volume, sports-drink formula, medication instruction, or transport decision from these sources.
- Do not infer that water, oral rehydration solution, sports drinks, salt, or salt tablets are interchangeable, appropriate for every person, or safe when consciousness, swallowing, vomiting, kidney disease, heart disease, or other clinical factors are unknown.
- Do not infer that a phone app can measure core temperature, recognize altered mental status, determine severity, or replace emergency dispatch, first responders, or clinical examination.
- Do not infer that occupational guidance applies unchanged to children, older adults, pregnant people, people with disabilities, people in vehicles, indoor settings, athletes, or people exposed to drugs/toxins.
- Do not infer that public first-aid pages authorize clinical claims, medical-device claims, diagnosis, treatment, or regulatory approval for LifeSafe.
- Do not infer that the WHO population estimates or global risk statements establish an individual’s probability of heat illness or a local heat-warning threshold. [3] [4]

## Focused clinician review questions

1. What minimum, jurisdiction-neutral wording should distinguish suspected heatstroke from heat exhaustion without implying a validated diagnosis?
2. Which neurological signs should trigger emergency escalation, and how should the app handle confusion, seizure, collapse, unresponsiveness, or inability to swallow?
3. Should any temperature threshold be displayed at all? If yes, which measurement method, units, device limitations, and jurisdictional evidence support it?
4. What is the clinically safe wording for oral fluids, including when to avoid them, when the person is vomiting or confused, and how to avoid implying a prescribed volume or electrolyte formula?
5. How should rapid cooling be described without creating a rigid protocol, unsafe immersion instruction, cold injury risk, or delay in calling emergency services?
6. What modifications are needed for infants, children, older adults, pregnancy, disability, chronic heart/kidney disease, medication use, intoxication, and exertional versus environmental heat illness?
7. How should the product route users to local emergency numbers and services without assuming 911, 999, 112, NHS 111, or another country’s system?
8. What evidence and governance are needed for translations, skin-color descriptions, accessibility, low-literacy language, and culturally appropriate wording?
9. How should related conditions such as exertional rhabdomyolysis, heat syncope, heat cramps, fever, and toxin-induced hyperthermia be signposted without expanding scope into unsupported diagnosis?
10. What clinical owner, review interval, versioning, adverse-event monitoring, and jurisdictional sign-off are required before any user-facing release?

## Language-review terms

Review translations and plain-language equivalents for: **heat illness**, **heat stress**, **heat exhaustion**, **heatstroke/heat stroke**, **exertional heat illness**, **hyperthermia**, **very high temperature**, **confusion**, **altered mental status**, **unusual behavior**, **loss of consciousness**, **seizure/fit**, **rapid breathing**, **cool place/shade**, **active cooling**, **cold-water immersion**, **wet sheet**, **fanning**, **cold packs**, **fluids/hydration**, **swallowing safely**, **recovery position**, **emergency medical help**, **heat syncope/fainting**, **heat cramps**, **heat rash**, and **rhabdomyolysis/dark urine**.

Language review should specifically test whether “hot, dry skin,” “sweating,” “pale,” “flushed,” and “red” descriptions are understandable and non-exclusionary across skin tones. It should also test whether “heatstroke” and “heat exhaustion” are reliably distinguished in each target language, and whether emergency-service wording is locally recognizable.
