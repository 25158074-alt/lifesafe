# Pregnancy and childbirth emergencies — draft evidence note

> **DRAFT RESEARCH ONLY — NOT USER-FACING ADVICE.** This note has not received clinical, legal, safety, localization, or product approval. It must not be treated as a diagnosis, triage instruction, treatment protocol, or a unified LifeSafe app protocol.
>
> **Evidence status:** DRAFT_UNVERIFIED (research synthesis for clinician and safety review; sources were read on 2026-11-09).

## Scope

This note covers publicly available evidence about warning signs and urgent-care context during pregnancy, labour/childbirth, and the postpartum period. It focuses on recognition and escalation concepts, not on diagnosis or treatment. It includes global maternal-health context from WHO; public warning-sign information from the US CDC; NHS information for England; a WHO handbook chapter hosted by the US National Library of Medicine; and UK professional patient information from the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG).

It does not establish a single international definition of “emergency,” a universal response time, a transport plan, a medication or procedure list, or a product decision tree. It does not address every emergency, including all trauma, domestic violence, mental-health crises, newborn emergencies, or local emergency-service arrangements.

## Source-supported findings

- WHO defines maternal health across pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period. It identifies excessive blood loss, infection, high blood pressure, unsafe abortion, and obstructed labour as common direct causes of maternal injury and death, with anaemia, malaria, and heart disease among indirect causes. WHO states that many maternal deaths are preventable with timely management by a skilled health professional in a supportive environment. These are global statements, not a home-triage protocol. [1]
- The CDC’s US “Urgent Maternal Warning Signs” page says warning signs can occur during pregnancy and in the year after delivery and may indicate a life-threatening condition. Its listed signs include severe or persistent headache, fainting or ongoing dizziness, vision changes, fever of 38°C or higher, marked face/hand swelling, thoughts of self-harm or harm to the baby, breathing difficulty, chest pain or fast/irregular heartbeat, severe vomiting with inability to keep fluids down, severe persistent abdominal pain, reduced or stopped fetal movement, vaginal bleeding or fluid leakage during pregnancy, heavy postpartum bleeding, and one-sided limb swelling/redness/pain. The CDC page also says the list is not exhaustive and advises contacting a health professional if something feels wrong or is uncertain. [2]
- The CDC page gives a source-specific postpartum bleeding description of “soaking through one or more pads in an hour,” and says clots larger than an egg or passage of tissue are warning signs. This wording is from a US educational resource and must not be silently generalized to every setting or used as a universal app threshold without clinician review. [2]
- NHS England information says pregnancy bleeding is not always serious but can require urgent treatment. It distinguishes spotting, light bleeding, and heavy bleeding, and links urgency to symptoms such as severe abdominal pain, shoulder pain, faintness/dizziness or loss of consciousness, and heavy bleeding. It directs people to maternity services, an early-pregnancy unit, NHS 111, or 999 depending on the situation. These service routes are England-specific. [3]
- NHS England’s premature-labour page defines premature labour as labour before 37 weeks and advises contacting a midwife or maternity unit for regular contractions/tightenings, period-type pains, an unusual backache, or a gush/trickle of vaginal fluid. It says assessment may include checks, tests, and monitoring, and notes that care choices depend on gestational age, infection or bleeding, local neonatal facilities, and the person’s wishes. It also identifies changes in fetal movement, bleeding, abdominal/back pain, contractions, feverishness, or greenish/smelly fluid as reasons for immediate contact after preterm pre-labour rupture of membranes. [4]
- WHO’s postpartum-haemorrhage page describes severe bleeding after childbirth as postpartum haemorrhage (PPH), calls it the leading cause of maternal mortality worldwide, and states that affected women may need urgent surgical intervention. The page links to WHO’s 2025 consolidated guidelines for prevention, diagnosis, and treatment in pregnancy, childbirth, and the immediate postpartum period. The page is a global clinical-policy context source; it does not provide a layperson protocol for managing bleeding at home. [5]
- The WHO counselling handbook chapter says all pregnant women, partners, and families should know danger signs and know where to seek skilled care. It lists vaginal bleeding, convulsions/fits, severe headache with blurred vision, fever with profound weakness, severe abdominal pain, and fast or difficult breathing as signs requiring immediate hospital or health-centre care in its counselling context. It emphasizes that sudden complications can occur in any pregnancy, that local words and cultural context matter, and that emergency planning should include location, transport, and support. It also warns against fear-based messaging and false reassurance. [6]
- RCOG’s early-pregnancy patient information says bleeding and/or pain in the first three months is common and does not always mean there is a problem, but medical advice should be sought. It identifies heavy bleeding, severe abdominal pain, shoulder pain, dizziness, and fainting as reasons to contact an early-pregnancy service or nearest A&E immediately. It explains that evaluation may require history, examination, ultrasound, urine and blood tests, and follow-up because ectopic pregnancy and other causes may not be resolved from symptoms alone. This is UK professional patient information focused on early pregnancy, not on all pregnancy or childbirth emergencies. [7]

## Population and jurisdiction limits

The sources address different populations and systems. WHO sources are global and intended to support health-system and professional work; they do not guarantee availability of skilled care, transport, blood, surgery, neonatal services, or emergency numbers in a particular country. The CDC source is US-focused and covers pregnancy through one year after delivery. NHS and RCOG sources are UK-focused, with NHS service instructions specific to England and RCOG guidance written for UK patient-information use. The NCBI-hosted handbook is a 2013 WHO counselling resource and may not reflect every later guideline update or local practice.

Definitions and triggers vary by gestational age, postpartum timing, suspected cause, baseline health, pregnancy type, and care setting. Access barriers, language, disability, age, gender identity, rurality, conflict, and safeguarding circumstances may materially alter how a person can seek care. No source reviewed here validates a single set of emergency numbers, service names, transport assumptions, or thresholds for all LifeSafe users.

## Disagreements and uncertainties

The sources overlap on bleeding, severe pain, neurological symptoms, breathing difficulty, and reduced fetal movement, but they do not use identical wording or escalation categories. For example, CDC lists any pregnancy bleeding beyond spotting as an urgent warning sign, NHS distinguishes bleeding patterns and routes urgency using additional symptoms, and RCOG focuses on early pregnancy and uses its own service pathways. These differences may reflect jurisdiction, audience, gestational stage, and purpose rather than a direct scientific contradiction.

The sources also differ in time windows: CDC extends warning signs to one year after delivery, WHO discusses the postnatal period and immediate postpartum care, while the NHS pages are condition- or stage-specific. They should not be collapsed into one universal postpartum window without clinician review.

The evidence reviewed does not verify a complete, current, globally representative list of childbirth emergencies or validate layperson recognition of each sign. It does not establish whether any specific symptom should trigger ambulance dispatch versus urgent maternity contact in every jurisdiction. It also does not establish the safety or effectiveness of an app’s symptom collection, translation, risk scoring, alerting, or referral logic.

## What must not be inferred

- Do not infer that a symptom list diagnoses haemorrhage, pre-eclampsia, ectopic pregnancy, infection, labour, or any other condition.
- Do not infer that absence of a listed warning sign means the pregnancy or postpartum period is safe.
- Do not combine the CDC, WHO, NHS, and RCOG lists into a single ranked or universal protocol.
- Do not transplant NHS 111/999, maternity-unit pathways, A&E routes, or any other country-specific service instruction into another jurisdiction.
- Do not treat a source-specific number, gestational cut-off, postpartum window, pad description, or movement statement as a universal threshold.
- Do not infer that home observation, self-treatment, medication, food/drink, physical positioning, or waiting is appropriate from these sources.
- Do not infer that a digital tool can replace skilled assessment, emergency dispatch, maternity triage, examination, ultrasound, laboratory testing, monitoring, surgery, transfusion, or neonatal care.
- Do not infer that WHO’s global cause statements or PPH policy pages approve any LifeSafe content, wording, workflow, or clinical claim.
- Do not infer that “normal,” “common,” or “not always serious” means a person should delay seeking care.
- Do not infer coverage for newborn emergencies, pregnancy loss, abortion-related complications, safeguarding, or mental-health emergencies beyond what a source explicitly addresses.

## Focused clinician review questions

1. Which pregnancy, labour, and postpartum populations and time windows should LifeSafe cover, and which should be explicitly out of scope?
2. For each proposed warning sign, what is the clinically defensible user wording, urgency level, and required uncertainty statement by gestational and postpartum stage?
3. Which signs require immediate emergency dispatch, same-day maternity assessment, or routine clinician contact in each target jurisdiction, and what authoritative local service source supports each route?
4. Should bleeding descriptions, fetal-movement concerns, fluid leakage, fever, pain, headache/vision changes, breathing/chest symptoms, seizures, mental-health concerns, and reduced consciousness be handled as separate pathways rather than one score?
5. What safeguards are needed for users who cannot access transport, are alone, have disability or language barriers, are minors, or face coercion or violence?
6. What current national guidelines should govern pregnancy-specific thresholds, and how will versioning and jurisdiction changes be maintained?
7. Which terms and translations are safe for lay use, including “waters breaking,” “fluid leakage,” “heavy bleeding,” “fetal movement,” “fainting,” “fits/seizures,” and “postpartum haemorrhage”?
8. What clinical and human-factors testing is required before any warning-sign content is shown to users, and what evidence would support or prohibit automated triage or reassurance?
9. What referral and escalation wording prevents false reassurance while avoiding fear-based messaging, stigma, or promises about outcomes?
10. What additional review is required for pregnancy loss, abortion-related emergencies, newborn compromise, and mental-health or self-harm risk?

## Language-review terms

Review these terms with clinicians, midwives, translators, and people with lived experience in each target language and jurisdiction: **pregnancy**, **childbirth/labour**, **postpartum/postnatal**, **maternity unit**, **early-pregnancy service**, **vaginal bleeding**, **spotting**, **heavy bleeding**, **fluid leakage**, **waters breaking**, **contractions/tightenings**, **period-type pain**, **severe abdominal pain**, **shoulder pain**, **fainting**, **dizziness**, **seizure/fit**, **fever**, **difficulty breathing**, **chest pain**, **headache with vision changes**, **swelling**, **baby’s movement**, **reduced or stopped movement**, **infection**, **ectopic pregnancy**, **miscarriage/pregnancy loss**, **postpartum haemorrhage**, **skilled health professional**, **urgent**, **immediate**, and **emergency**. Check whether local terms distinguish “call emergency services now” from “contact a maternity service urgently,” and avoid assuming that “labour,” “delivery,” “birth,” “postnatal,” and “postpartum” are interchangeable for every audience.
