# Road traffic accident / multiple trauma — draft evidence note

> **DRAFT RESEARCH ONLY — NOT USER-FACING ADVICE.** This note is not clinical approval, a treatment guideline, or a validated LifeSafe protocol. It must not be used to prescribe a unified app response. Any product wording, triage logic, localization, or clinical claims require focused clinician, legal, safeguarding, accessibility, and jurisdictional review.
>
> **Evidence status:** DRAFT_UNVERIFIED. Sources were opened and read on 2026-11-09. This note is a research synthesis, not an exhaustive systematic review.

## Scope

This topic concerns road traffic crashes/collisions in which a person may have multiple traumatic injuries, including potentially life-threatening bleeding, airway or breathing problems, head or spinal injury, shock, burns, crush injury, or more than one casualty. The scope is limited to publicly available evidence relevant to the scale and consequences of road traffic injury, post-crash care, and principles described by recognized first-aid organizations for a bystander or first-aid provider.

It does **not** establish a diagnosis, define “major trauma” for every health system, or specify a single sequence of actions for an app. It does not cover professional trauma-team care, extrication, transport destination decisions, medication administration, safeguarding investigations, or post-traumatic mental-health treatment in sufficient detail.

## Source-supported findings

### Burden and urgency

WHO reports that approximately 1.16 million people die each year from road traffic crashes and that a further 20–50 million sustain non-fatal injuries, many with disability. WHO identifies road traffic injury as the leading cause of death for people aged 5–29 years, with substantial differences by country income group, region, age, sex, and road-user type. These are global estimates and should not be treated as a local incidence estimate. [1]

WHO also states that delays in detecting and providing care after a crash can increase injury severity, and that post-crash care is time-sensitive. The page frames improvement as requiring timely prehospital care and appropriate prehospital and hospital systems, rather than a standalone bystander intervention. [1]

### Bystander first-aid principles described by a current recognized guideline

The Resuscitation Council UK’s 2025 First Aid Guidelines describe first aid as help provided by anyone in an emergency, within the provider’s competence and limits, while seeking additional help. Its key points include checking scene safety, calling emergency services early, and using equipment or medication only when trained to do so. It recommends a structured ABCDE assessment for identifying and treating life-threatening conditions, while emphasizing that the framework is guidance for first-aid providers and not a substitute for emergency services or professional assessment. [2]

For life-threatening bleeding, the same guideline describes an escalating approach beginning with manual direct pressure and then, where appropriate, haemostatic dressings and/or tourniquets. The guideline’s text is not evidence that every casualty, wound, setting, device, or rescuer should receive the same intervention. It also states that people with decreased responsiveness who do not meet CPR criteria may be placed in a lateral recovery position, but that in trauma or agonal breathing the person should not be moved into that position. These distinctions underscore why a generic “recovery position” or bleeding instruction cannot safely be generalized without clinical design review. [2]

### Training, context, and limitations of first-aid resources

St John Ambulance presents its online material as a library covering common conditions and emergency first aid, including CPR, recovery position, fractures, head injury, and other conditions. It states that its clinical team includes qualified healthcare professionals and that its advice is intended to help people learn first-aid techniques. This is an educational resource, not a jurisdiction-neutral trauma system or an authorization for untrained users to perform advanced procedures. [3]

The Resuscitation Council UK guideline explicitly says first-aid courses should be tailored to participants’ needs and settings, including low-resource and rural settings, and should account for sociocultural appropriateness, feasibility, and fear or distress among lay rescuers. This supports localization and usability review, but does not establish which content, language, emergency number, equipment, or thresholds are appropriate in any particular country. [2]

### Multiple people and the wider post-collision population

A 2025 UK Department for Transport–commissioned evidence review describes people affected by road traffic collisions as including direct victims, family members, carers, friends, and witnesses. It reports that physical, psychological, emotional, and financial effects can continue beyond the immediate collision. The report is focused on post-collision support in England and Wales, not acute clinical management. [4]

The same review notes that the police-based “seriously injured” classification in the UK STATS19 context generally does not reflect a medical examination and may be influenced by hospitalization or information available soon after the collision. This is a material warning against using administrative severity labels as if they were clinical diagnoses or universal trauma thresholds. [4]

## Population and jurisdiction limits

- WHO’s burden figures and risk-factor statements are global and aggregate. They do not provide a LifeSafe-user denominator, country-specific incidence, or a validated individual risk prediction. [1]
- The Resuscitation Council UK guidance is a UK professional-organization guideline. Its emergency-service reference, training assumptions, terminology, and recommendations may not map directly to other countries or to users without training. [2]
- St John Ambulance’s material is UK-based public first-aid education. Its emergency numbers, wording, and educational scope require localization before any reuse. [3]
- The Department for Transport–commissioned review covers England and Wales and concerns support after road traffic collisions. Its definitions and service landscape should not be generalized to other jurisdictions. [4]
- None of these sources validates a consumer app’s ability to recognize multiple trauma, determine casualty priority, assess spinal injury, or direct treatment safely in real time.

## Disagreements and uncertainties

The sources do not provide one universally accepted public-facing algorithm for road-crash multiple trauma. The RCUK guideline gives principles and condition-specific recommendations, while WHO addresses system-level post-crash care and the DfT review addresses broader post-collision support. They operate at different levels and should not be merged into a single protocol without clinical governance.

Important uncertainties remain about lay-recognizable signs of occult internal injury, spinal injury, traumatic brain injury, shock, and crush injury; how guidance should change for children, pregnancy, older adults, disability, anticoagulant use, or multiple casualties; and how to adapt advice where emergency response times, equipment, language, or legal protections differ. The reviewed public pages do not establish app-ready thresholds for these situations.

The fetched NHS first-aid URL did not yield a reliable directly readable NHS page in this research pass, so NHS evidence is not counted or cited here. No claim requiring NHS-specific wording is made.

## What must not be inferred

- Do not infer that a road crash automatically means a particular injury, diagnosis, or severity category.
- Do not infer that a conscious, talking person is free of life-threatening injury or that visible bleeding is the only urgent problem.
- Do not infer that ABCDE, direct pressure, a tourniquet, recovery position, movement restriction, extrication, or any other measure is suitable for every casualty or rescuer.
- Do not infer that “serious injury” in police or administrative data equals a clinical major-trauma definition.
- Do not infer that global WHO estimates apply to a specific country, road, population, or individual.
- Do not infer that first-aid educational content is clinical approval, legal authorization, or a substitute for emergency-dispatch instructions.
- Do not infer that guidance from the UK can be used unchanged in another jurisdiction, especially for emergency numbers, terminology, equipment, scope of practice, or language.
- Do not infer a unified app protocol, automated triage threshold, or safe instruction ordering from this note.

## Focused clinician review questions

1. What minimum clinical scope should “road traffic accident / multiple trauma” cover, and which presentations must be explicitly excluded or escalated?
2. Which signs can a lay user reasonably observe and report without examination, and which terms risk false reassurance or dangerous delay?
3. How should advice differ for a single casualty versus multiple casualties, and how should the app avoid directing a user into traffic, fire, electrical, chemical, water, or unstable-vehicle hazards?
4. What wording is safe for severe bleeding, suspected spinal or head injury, impaired consciousness, abnormal breathing, burns, crush injury, and shock while awaiting dispatch instructions?
5. When, if ever, can movement, recovery positioning, tourniquet use, or other interventions be mentioned, and what training, equipment, and dispatcher context are prerequisites?
6. How should content handle children, pregnancy, frailty, disability, anticoagulants, language barriers, hearing or vision impairment, and users who are themselves injured?
7. What country-specific emergency numbers, dispatch pathways, Good Samaritan/legal considerations, and referral destinations must be configured rather than assumed?
8. What evidence, testing, human-factors review, and incident-monitoring plan would be required before any user-facing release?

## Language-review terms

Terms requiring clinician and localization review include: “road traffic accident” versus “road traffic collision” versus “crash”; “multiple trauma” versus “major trauma” versus “polytrauma”; “casualty,” “injured person,” and “victim”; “life-threatening bleeding”; “severe bleeding”; “shock”; “unresponsive”; “not breathing normally”; “spinal injury”; “neck injury”; “head injury”; “crush injury”; “extrication”; “recovery position”; “direct pressure”; “haemostatic dressing”; “tourniquet”; “emergency services”; “ambulance”; “dispatcher”; “bystander”; and “first-aid provider.” Review must include plain-language comprehension, translation, regional terminology, disability access, and avoidance of blame or stigma.
