# Severe dehydration in children — draft evidence note

> **DRAFT RESEARCH ONLY — NOT USER-FACING ADVICE.**  
> This note is not clinical approval, a diagnosis, triage guidance, or a substitute for local emergency instructions. It must not be used to prescribe a unified LifeSafe app protocol. Any product wording, decision logic, thresholds, escalation language, or treatment content requires focused clinician, safeguarding, localization, and regulatory review.
>
> **Evidence checked:** public pages opened and read on 2026-11-09. Source currency and page status can change after this date.

## Scope

This note concerns recognition and clinical significance of **severe dehydration in children**, especially when associated with vomiting, diarrhoea, reduced intake, fever, or other fluid losses. It summarizes what the reviewed public sources say about risk groups, observed signs, the need for urgent assessment, and the broad distinction between enteral rehydration for some less-severe cases and hospital-based treatment for severe dehydration or shock.

It does not establish a single definition for all children, causes, ages, comorbidities, countries, or care settings. It does not provide a dosage schedule, home-treatment recipe, emergency call script, or app decision tree.

## Source-supported findings

- Dehydration is a net loss of fluid. Babies and young children are at increased risk; the reviewed explanations include low body weight, dependence on caregivers for fluids, and inability to reliably communicate thirst or illness. Chronic heart or kidney disease, some gastrointestinal conditions, metabolic disease, and diuretic use may increase risk or complicate assessment. [1] [2] [3]

- The reviewed public health sources describe concerning observations that can occur with dehydration in children, including reduced urination or fewer wet nappies, dark urine, dry mouth or lips, few or no tears, sunken eyes, drowsiness or lethargy, rapid breathing or fast heart rate, cold extremities, and pale, mottled, blue, or grey skin. These signs are not interchangeable, and their meaning depends on age, illness, examination, and context. [1] [2] [3] [4]

- Severe dehydration is treated as time-critical in the reviewed guidance. WHO states that diarrhoeal illness can require intravenous fluids when severe dehydration or shock is present and recommends professional assessment when there are signs of dehydration. NHS guidance directs urgent help for serious-dehydration signs and emergency help for features that may indicate shock, such as altered consciousness, difficulty breathing, cold skin, or blue/grey/pale/blotchy colour. [1] [2]

- The Royal Children’s Hospital (RCH) clinical guideline states that clinical signs can estimate severity but are often imprecise; serial weights are the best measure of acute change when reliable pre-illness weight is available. Its table describes severe dehydration as a clinical category with markedly abnormal signs and gives “≥10%” in that local guideline’s percentage column. It also states that shock requires prompt intravenous fluid resuscitation and that sepsis must be considered. [4]

- RCH’s clinical guideline prefers oral or nasogastric rehydration for mild or moderate dehydration, while stating that intravenous rehydration is required in severe dehydration or when enteral rehydration cannot be tolerated. The CDC review likewise describes severe dehydration as a medical emergency requiring immediate intravenous rehydration and close monitoring. These are clinical-care statements, not instructions for an app or a layperson to deliver intravenous treatment. [4] [5]

- For diarrhoea, WHO identifies low-osmolarity oral rehydration salts (ORS) as a proven treatment for children and says rehydration may require intravenous fluids in severe dehydration or shock. WHO also emphasizes continued nutrient-rich feeding and professional assessment when dehydration signs are present. UNICEF describes ORS as a salt-and-sugar preparation dissolved in clean water for fluid-loss illnesses, with its page focused on childhood diarrhoea supply and procurement rather than individualized diagnosis. [1] [6]

- NHS and RCH public information both describe offering small, frequent amounts of suitable fluid in some vomiting/dehydration situations and continuing age-appropriate feeding or breast/formula feeding, but their examples and operational details are jurisdiction- and context-specific. These statements should not be generalized to severe dehydration, altered consciousness, shock, aspiration risk, electrolyte disorders, or children with complex medical conditions. [2] [3]

## Population and jurisdiction limits

The sources cover different populations and purposes. WHO and UNICEF material is global and principally addresses diarrhoeal disease and public-health treatment. NHS and NHS inform pages are for the United Kingdom, with UK service routes such as NHS 111 and 999/A&E. RCH material is from an Australian tertiary paediatric service in Victoria; its clinical guideline is directed at health professionals and its Kids Health Info page is written for families. The CDC material is a United States review/guideline document and is older than the other reviewed web pages, although it remains publicly available.

Age boundaries are not uniform. Some sources distinguish babies, infants, children under five, and older children; RCH specifically flags infants under six months as higher risk. A finding from one age group must not be silently applied to neonates, young infants, adolescents, or children with prematurity, malnutrition, disability, or complex disease.

The evidence reviewed is strongest for dehydration in the context of acute gastroenteritis/diarrhoea and fluid loss. It does not establish a complete approach to dehydration caused by diabetic ketoacidosis, burns, sepsis, respiratory illness, heat illness, renal disease, cardiac disease, toxic ingestion, or other causes.

## Disagreements and uncertainties

- **Severity thresholds:** RCH provides a local clinical table with severe dehydration at ≥10% estimated fluid loss, while the NHS public pages use symptom-based urgent/emergency signs rather than a percentage. Percentage estimates can be unavailable or inaccurate without a trustworthy recent weight; RCH explicitly says clinical signs are often imprecise. No single percentage should be treated as a universally validated lay threshold.

- **Sign combinations and urgency:** Sources overlap on lethargy, reduced urine, sunken eyes, cold or mottled extremities, and abnormal breathing, but they do not present identical combinations or identical service pathways. The NHS separates urgent advice from emergency signs suggestive of shock; RCH’s parent page uses Australian emergency routing. These pathways cannot be merged without a selected jurisdiction and clinical review.

- **Choice of fluids outside severe cases:** WHO emphasizes ORS for diarrhoeal disease. NHS and RCH family pages provide different examples of fluids and feeding practices, with age and illness qualifications. The reviewed sources do not support a universal home-fluid instruction for every child or every cause of dehydration.

- **Clinical assessment:** A child’s mental state, perfusion, breathing, pulse, blood pressure, capillary refill, weight change, intake/output, comorbidities, and likely cause may change classification and treatment. Caregiver-observed signs alone cannot reliably distinguish dehydration from sepsis, metabolic disease, or other shock states.

- **WHO module access:** The specific WHO “Module 4” PDF search result was not readable through the page-extraction route used here (the endpoint returned only “DSpace”), so its detailed plan tables were not used as evidence in this note. The accessible WHO diarrhoea topic page was read directly and supports the narrower claims cited above. No claim here depends on an unread snippet.

## What must not be inferred

- Do not infer that a symptom checklist can diagnose severe dehydration, quantify fluid deficit, or rule out sepsis, diabetic ketoacidosis, electrolyte disturbance, poisoning, or another emergency.
- Do not infer that absence of one sign excludes severe illness. Signs vary by age, cause, timing, baseline health, skin colour, and observer reliability.
- Do not infer that a percentage threshold, ORS amount, IV fluid amount, or reassessment interval from one jurisdiction is a globally applicable rule.
- Do not infer that ORS is appropriate as sole management for a child with shock, markedly altered consciousness, inability to safely swallow, severe electrolyte abnormality, or another condition requiring clinician-directed care.
- Do not infer that family-facing examples of water, diluted drinks, juice, breast milk, formula, or other fluids are interchangeable, suitable for all ages, or suitable for severe dehydration. Formula must not be diluted based on the reviewed NHS guidance, but this note does not turn that statement into a universal treatment protocol.
- Do not infer that a child who temporarily drinks, urinates, or appears better is safe without considering ongoing losses, recurrence, underlying disease, or clinician advice.
- Do not infer that this evidence note authorizes LifeSafe wording, triage logic, notification behavior, treatment recommendation, or emergency-service localization.

## Focused clinician review questions

1. What definition of “severe dehydration” should be used for each target age band, and should any percentage estimate be shown to users at all?
2. Which caregiver-observable signs should trigger emergency action, urgent same-day assessment, or routine clinical review in the target jurisdiction, and how should shock, sepsis, breathing difficulty, altered consciousness, and blue/grey colour be handled?
3. How should the product handle infants under six months, premature infants, children with malnutrition or disability, and children with heart, kidney, metabolic, gastrointestinal, or other chronic disease?
4. What language accurately communicates that clinical signs are imprecise and that absence of a sign does not exclude dangerous illness?
5. Under what clinician-approved circumstances, if any, may a public-facing product mention ORS, continued feeding, breastfeeding/formula, or small frequent sips—and what exclusions and localization are required?
6. Which local emergency number, urgent-care route, and service terminology are correct for each deployment country, and what fallback wording is safe when location is unknown?
7. What is the approved escalation wording for a child who cannot keep fluids down, is difficult to wake, has reduced urine, has cold/mottled skin, breathes abnormally, or may have shock?
8. What safeguarding, accessibility, translation, and caregiver-capacity review is required so that “monitoring” language does not delay care?
9. What source-update process, owner, review interval, and change-control record will keep any approved content current?

## Language-review terms

Review these terms with clinicians and language specialists before any user-facing use: **dehydration; severe dehydration; serious dehydration; fluid loss; oral rehydration solution (ORS); electrolyte; lethargy; drowsy; difficult to wake; reduced consciousness; shock; cold hands/feet; mottled or blotchy skin; sunken eyes; sunken fontanelle/soft spot; fewer wet nappies; reduced urination; unable to keep fluids down; urgent medical help; emergency department; ambulance; NHS 111/999; local emergency number.**

Check that translations preserve urgency without implying a diagnosis, distinguish “sleepy” from “difficult to wake,” avoid stigmatizing or culturally narrow descriptions of skin colour, and use the locally understood terms for urine, nappies/diapers, oral rehydration solution, and emergency services.
