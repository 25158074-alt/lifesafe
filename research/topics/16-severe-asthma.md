# Severe asthma or breathing difficulty — draft evidence note

> **Draft research only. Not user-facing advice. Not clinical approval.** This note summarizes public source material for clinician and safety review. It does **not** prescribe a unified LifeSafe app protocol, triage algorithm, medication instruction, or emergency-service workflow.
>
> **Access date for all sources:** 2026-11-09.

## Scope

This note covers publicly available guidance relevant to a person with a possible severe asthma attack or acute breathing difficulty, with emphasis on recognition of deterioration, positioning/reassurance, use of the person's own prescribed inhaler, and escalation to emergency services. It does not establish diagnosis, distinguish asthma from other causes of breathlessness, specify a globally valid medicine dose, or define a production-ready app experience.

## Source-supported findings

- The NHS describes an asthma attack as severe symptoms with difficulty breathing and notes that it can be life-threatening. It lists wheeze, cough, shortness of breath, and chest tightness as asthma symptoms. [1]
- The NHS advises a person having an asthma attack to sit up straight and try to stay calm. Its emergency wording says to call 999 if the person is worsening, does not improve after the maximum dose using their asthma reliever, or does not have an inhaler; it also says not to drive oneself to A&E. [1]
- The NHS page distinguishes inhaler regimens. For a blue reliever inhaler it gives one puff every 30–60 seconds up to 10 puffs; for an AIR or MART inhaler it gives one puff every 1–3 minutes up to 6 puffs. It explicitly says that a personal asthma action plan with a different maximum dose should be followed. These are source-specific instructions, not a universal rule for an app. [1]
- The NHS says that if symptoms are no better after 10 minutes and an ambulance has not arrived, the inhaler may be used again up to the same maximum dose, and says the emergency call handler will advise what to do. [1]
- The British Red Cross describes an attack as narrowed airways that make breathing difficult. It identifies possible difficulty breathing and speaking, cough, wheeze, anxiety/distress, and in some cases greyish-blue lips, earlobes, or nail beds. [2]
- The British Red Cross advises helping the person sit comfortably, helping them take their inhaler, and reassuring them. It says to call 999 if the attack becomes severe, the inhaler is unavailable or has no effect, the person is worsening, or they become unable to talk; it also says not to leave them because the attack can become severe quickly. [2]
- The British Red Cross gives additional escalation examples: a first attack, stopping breathing, difficulty talking or making a noise, exhaustion, no relief from the inhaler, no asthma medication, or worsening. It directs responders to separate first-aid guidance if the person stops breathing. [2]
- The WHO describes asthma as a chronic lung disease affecting children and adults, with symptoms that can be mild or severe and can come and go. It says severe symptoms may require emergency care and hospital treatment/monitoring, and that asthma symptoms can also be caused by other conditions. [3]
- The WHO emphasizes that inhaler access and treatment availability vary between countries and resource settings. It describes asthma action plans and health-professional education as part of management, but its fact sheet is not a local emergency dispatch protocol. [3]

## Population and jurisdiction limits

The NHS and British Red Cross pages are UK-oriented. Their emergency number, 999, and references to A&E, GP review, NHS 111, and UK medicines/inhaler terminology must not be assumed to apply outside the UK. The NHS page addresses adults and children in general but does not, on the cited page, provide a complete age-, pregnancy-, comorbidity-, or device-specific protocol. The British Red Cross page is explicitly adult first aid, while its page separately links to child guidance; adult wording should not automatically be applied to children or babies. [1] [2]

The WHO source is global and covers broad disease burden, symptoms, treatment access, and health-system context. It does not supply a country-specific emergency number or a complete first-aid sequence. Its global statements should not be read as evidence that every person has access to an inhaler, spacer, ambulance, or hospital care. [3]

## Disagreements and uncertainties

The sources do not provide one universally interchangeable inhaler instruction. The NHS separates blue reliever inhalers from AIR/MART inhalers and defers to a personal asthma action plan; the British Red Cross uses more general reliever-inhaler wording and also references one or two puffs every 30–60 seconds up to 10 puffs. This difference may reflect device, medicine, age, local guidance, and action-plan context. It requires clinician review before any wording is operationalized. [1] [2]

The sources use overlapping but non-identical danger indicators. The NHS centers worsening and failure to improve after the maximum dose or no inhaler; the British Red Cross additionally names first attack, inability to talk, exhaustion, stopping breathing, and visible grey-blue color. These should not be collapsed into a validated severity score without clinical review. [1] [2]

Breathing difficulty is not specific to asthma. WHO explicitly notes that asthma-like symptoms can have other causes. A person, caregiver, or app cannot safely infer asthma solely from wheeze, cough, shortness of breath, chest tightness, or distress. [3]

The cited pages are public educational guidance, not evidence of LifeSafe clinical safety, effectiveness, regulatory status, or local medical-service integration. No claim about app outcomes, user comprehension, or clinical approval is supported by these sources.

## What must not be inferred

- Do not infer that any breathing difficulty is asthma or that asthma is the only emergency explanation.
- Do not infer that “blue inhaler” identifies the correct medicine, dose, device, or user population everywhere.
- Do not infer that NHS dosing can be generalized to every inhaler, age group, pregnancy, comorbidity, country, or personal action plan.
- Do not infer that a first-aid app can replace an emergency call, dispatcher instructions, a prescribed asthma action plan, or professional assessment.
- Do not infer that absence of wheeze means the situation is safe, or that visible lip/nail color change is required before escalation.
- Do not infer that a person should drive themselves to hospital, or that 999 is the correct emergency number outside the UK.
- Do not infer that inhaler assistance, spacer use, CPR, or care for an unresponsive person can be safely compressed into one universal instruction without age-, device-, and jurisdiction-specific review.
- Do not infer that a source’s public wording constitutes clinical, legal, regulatory, or product approval.

## Focused clinician review questions

1. Which target populations and jurisdictions, if any, are in scope for LifeSafe’s severe-asthma content? How should emergency numbers and service names be localized?
2. What clinician-approved wording distinguishes a suspected asthma attack from undifferentiated severe breathing difficulty without encouraging self-diagnosis?
3. Which signs should trigger immediate emergency contact, and should inability to speak, exhaustion, cyanosis/grey-blue color, first attack, collapse, or absent breathing be handled as separate urgent branches?
4. How should the product defer to a user’s personal asthma action plan, including when that plan conflicts with generic public guidance?
5. Which inhaler/device categories may be mentioned, and what medication, age, pregnancy, spacer, or comorbidity exclusions are required?
6. What is the approved boundary between helping someone access/use their own prescribed medicine and giving medication instructions?
7. What should the app say when the person has no inhaler, the inhaler is unavailable or ineffective, symptoms worsen, or the person becomes unresponsive?
8. What accessibility and language testing is needed for distressed users, children, caregivers, people with hearing/speech disability, and people with limited health literacy?
9. What local emergency-service and safeguarding review is required before any deployment claim or clinical sign-off?

## Language-review terms

Review the terms **asthma attack**, **severe asthma**, **breathing difficulty**, **shortness of breath**, **wheeze**, **chest tightness**, **unable to talk**, **exhaustion**, **grey-blue/blue lips or nail beds**, **reliever inhaler**, **blue inhaler**, **AIR**, **MART**, **spacer**, **personal asthma action plan**, **emergency services**, **ambulance**, **999**, **A&E**, **unresponsive**, and **not breathing**. Check whether each term is understandable, translated accurately, culturally appropriate, and safe for the intended age group and jurisdiction. Avoid implying that a color, brand-like label, or symptom is universal.
