# Draft evidence note: Low blood sugar in a person with diabetes

> **DRAFT RESEARCH ONLY — NOT USER-FACING ADVICE, NOT CLINICAL APPROVAL, AND NOT A UNIFIED LIFESAFE APP PROTOCOL.**
>
> This note records what selected public sources say. It does not establish a product rule, clinical pathway, diagnosis, or emergency-service instruction. Any implementation or user-facing wording requires qualified clinician, safeguarding, medical-device, legal, and jurisdictional review.

## Scope

This note covers publicly available information about low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia) in a person with diabetes, including recognition, broad first-aid/self-care concepts, severe episodes, and escalation. It is limited to four sources read directly on 2026-11-09: the UK National Health Service (NHS), the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the British Red Cross. It does not assess medication labels, professional guidelines, ambulance-service protocols, paediatric pathways, pregnancy, inpatient care, driving law in every jurisdiction, or diagnosis of hypoglycaemia in people without diabetes.

## Source-supported findings

### Recognition and terminology

The NHS describes a hypo as usually blood glucose below 4 mmol/L and lists hunger, dizziness, anxiety or irritability, sweating, shaking, tingling lips, palpitations, tiredness or weakness, blurred vision, and confusion among possible symptoms. It says severe low blood sugar may involve a seizure or unconsciousness. [1]

The CDC uses below 70 mg/dL as low blood sugar and describes severe low blood sugar as below 54 mg/dL on its overview page. It lists fast heartbeat, shaking, sweating, anxiety, irritability or confusion, dizziness, and hunger, and notes that symptoms vary and hypoglycaemia unawareness can occur. [2]

### Broad treatment concepts for an alert person who can swallow

The NHS says a person with diabetes who has symptoms or a reading below 4 mmol/L should take something that raises glucose quickly, recheck after 10–15 minutes, repeat if still below 4 mmol/L, and then take longer-lasting food once symptoms improve and the glucose is above 4 mmol/L. Its examples include fruit juice, a sugary non-diet drink, glucose/dextrose tablets, jelly babies, or glucose gel. [1]

The CDC presents a 15-15 rule: take 15 grams of carbohydrate, wait 15 minutes, recheck, and repeat if still below 70 mg/dL; it then advises a balanced snack or meal containing protein and carbohydrate. It cautions that high-fibre and high-fat foods can slow sugar absorption and gives US serving examples. It also explicitly notes that young children may need less than 15 grams and that the clinician should determine the amount. [3]

These are source-specific examples, not a basis for selecting one universal quantity, food list, measurement unit, recheck interval, or target for LifeSafe.

### Severe episode and inability to swallow

The NHS says not to give food or drink to an unconscious person because they cannot swallow safely; it advises the recovery position, glucagon if available and the helper knows how to use it, staying with the person, and emergency calling in specified circumstances. [1]

The CDC says severe low blood sugar may prevent self-treatment, identifies injectable glucagon as the best treatment for severely low blood sugar, and says emergency medical treatment should be contacted immediately after glucagon. It says that if the person is awake and able to swallow, fast-acting sugar should be followed by longer-acting carbohydrate with protein. [3]

The British Red Cross gives adult first-aid advice: help the person rest and give a sugary drink or food, reassure them, give more sugary food if they respond well, and call 999 if they do not improve. For an unresponsive person it directs the reader to separate unresponsive-person first-aid guidance, rather than specifying a single diabetes protocol. [4]

### Causes, recurrence, and follow-up

The NHS identifies insulin and some other diabetes medicines as causes and lists excess insulin, missed or delayed meals, exercise without sufficient carbohydrate or insulin adjustment, and alcohol, particularly without food, as risk factors. It advises clinical review for frequent, severe, or nocturnal hypos and for impaired symptom recognition. [1]

The CDC lists medication dose/timing, carbohydrate intake, physical activity, alcohol, meal composition, weather, schedule changes, altitude, puberty, and menstruation among possible contributors. It advises discussing repeated episodes and not changing medicines without talking to a clinician. [2] [3]

## Population and jurisdiction limits

The NHS and British Red Cross pages are UK public-health/first-aid information and refer to UK emergency numbers (999), UK-style units and examples, and UK services such as a GP, diabetes care team, and NHS 111. [1] [4] The CDC pages are US federal public-health information and use mg/dL, US serving examples, and a US-facing care context. [2] [3]

The British Red Cross page is explicitly adult first-aid advice. [4] The CDC says children may need a different carbohydrate amount, especially infants and toddlers. [3] None of these four pages is sufficient to define a paediatric, pregnancy, older-adult, cognitive-impairment, medication-specific, inpatient, or device-specific pathway.

The pages are public education resources, not evidence that a particular LifeSafe interface, alert, threshold, wording, or action has been clinically validated or approved. No source reviewed here establishes a global emergency number or a globally valid glucagon product/use instruction.

## Disagreements and uncertainties

* **Low threshold:** NHS uses below 4 mmol/L. CDC uses below 70 mg/dL (approximately 3.9 mmol/L), but conversion and rounding should not be silently treated as identical clinical rules. [1] [2] [3]
* **Severe threshold:** CDC’s overview says below 54 mg/dL, while its treatment page says below 55 mg/dL. The NHS page defines severe hypo by severe symptoms and loss of consciousness rather than giving a numeric severe threshold. [1] [2] [3]
* **Recheck and glucagon recovery timing:** NHS says recheck after 10–15 minutes and calls 999 if there is no recovery within 10 minutes after glucagon in its stated circumstances. CDC uses 15 minutes in its glucagon discussion and directs emergency treatment immediately after injection. These statements should not be collapsed into one timer. [1] [3]
* **Food examples:** CDC warns that fat and fibre can slow absorption, whereas the British Red Cross includes chocolate among sugary foods. The sources therefore do not support a single universal food ranking without clinical and wording review. [3] [4]
* **Recognition is imperfect:** Symptoms vary, and hypoglycaemia unawareness can occur. A symptom-only or reading-only design assumption would not be supported by these sources alone. [2]
* **Source currency and review:** The NHS page states it was last reviewed 03 August 2023 and next review due 03 August 2026; the CDC pages show May 2024 dates. The NHS page’s displayed next-review date has passed as of the requested access date, so currentness should be checked before reliance. [1] [2] [3]

## What must not be inferred

Do not infer that a single threshold, carbohydrate amount, recheck interval, recovery timer, food list, glucose unit, emergency number, or glucagon instruction is safe or appropriate for every person, age group, treatment, device, or country.

Do not infer that a person who is confused, drowsy, seizing, vomiting, unconscious, or otherwise unable to swallow can safely receive food, drink, tablets, or gel by mouth. Do not infer that a normal-looking or absent symptom rules out low blood sugar.

Do not infer that these sources approve LifeSafe, validate an app algorithm, authorize remote diagnosis, replace local emergency services, or justify medication changes. Do not infer that public first-aid wording resolves the separate clinical, safeguarding, accessibility, localization, or medical-device requirements of a digital product.

## Focused clinician review questions

1. What target population and episode contexts are in scope, and which must be excluded or separately handled (children, pregnancy, older adults, cognitive impairment, type 1/type 2 diabetes, insulin and non-insulin medicines, alcohol, exercise, and nocturnal episodes)?
2. Which definitions and units, if any, should be displayed, and how should the product handle meter, CGM, symptoms, trend arrows, delayed readings, sensor failure, and conflicting inputs?
3. What clinically reviewed distinction should be made between an alert person who can swallow, a person who is confused or deteriorating, and a person who is unable to swallow or unresponsive?
4. What locally correct emergency escalation wording and numbers apply in each launch jurisdiction, and what must happen when location is unknown?
5. What are the approved instructions and safeguards for glucagon, including product form, prescription/access assumptions, helper training, contraindications, and post-event evaluation?
6. How should the product handle repeated, nocturnal, unrecognised, medication-related, exercise-related, alcohol-related, or unresolved episodes without advising medication changes?
7. What usability, language, accessibility, safeguarding, and human-factors testing is required before any wording is shown to users?
8. What regulatory classification, clinical evaluation, incident reporting, and post-market monitoring obligations could apply to the intended feature?

## Language-review terms

Review the plain-language use and translation of: **low blood sugar**, **hypoglycaemia/hypoglycemia**, **blood glucose**, **reading**, **symptoms**, **severe**, **confused**, **drowsy**, **unresponsive**, **seizure/fit**, **can swallow safely**, **do not give food or drink**, **recovery position**, **glucagon**, **fast-acting carbohydrate/sugar**, **longer-lasting carbohydrate**, **non-diet drink**, **recheck**, **target range**, **hypoglycaemia unawareness**, **call emergency services**, and the local emergency number.

Check that the wording does not imply that all people have warning symptoms, that all sugary foods act at the same speed, that a numeric threshold is universal, or that an app can determine swallowing safety or diagnose the cause. Localize spelling, units, food examples, emergency numbers, health-service names, and culturally understandable terms only after jurisdiction-specific clinical review.
