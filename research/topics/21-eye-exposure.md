# LifeSafe evidence note — chemical exposure to the eye

> **DRAFT RESEARCH ONLY — NOT USER-FACING ADVICE.** This note is not clinical approval, a treatment protocol, or a recommendation to implement a unified LifeSafe app flow. It records public-source findings for clinician, safety, localization, and product review.
>
> **Research date/access date:** 2026-11-09

## Scope

This note covers first-aid and escalation information for a chemical splash or other chemical contact with the eye. It is intentionally limited to publicly available guidance from the NHS, CDC/NIOSH, Mayo Clinic, and the American Red Cross. It does not establish diagnosis, chemical-specific management, occupational compliance, emergency-dispatch wording, or a complete protocol for children, contact-lens users, vulnerable people, or people with severe injury. It also does not cover a foreign object in the eye except where a cited source discusses eye injury generally.

## Source-supported findings

- The NHS treats a strong chemical in the eye (examples given: oven cleaner or bleach) as an emergency: go to A&E or call 999, keep rinsing with water while waiting for medical help, and take the product container because its information may help treatment. [1]
- The NHS gives general eye-washing instructions of clean, not-hot water; holding the eye open; and running lots of water over the eyeball for at least 20 minutes, with a flow that is not too strong. On the same page it distinguishes minor irritants such as shampoo or dust from chemical injury and says to obtain medical help for a chemical in the eye. [1]
- CDC/NIOSH's first-aid table states that when a chemical contacts the eyes, the eyes should be irrigated immediately with large amounts of water, occasionally lifting the lower and upper lids, and medical attention should be obtained immediately. Its separate frostbite entry says that if eye tissue is not frozen, the eyes should be flushed thoroughly with large amounts of water for at least 15 minutes, again lifting the lids occasionally; persistent irritation, pain, swelling, tearing, or light sensitivity warrants medical attention as soon as possible. [2]
- Mayo Clinic states that a chemical splash in the eye requires immediate medical help and advises emergency care by an ophthalmologist or calling 911/the local emergency number. It advises taking the chemical container or chemical name to the emergency provider. The page also says not to rub the eye, not to put anything except water or contact-lens saline rinse in the eye, and not to use eye drops unless emergency personnel direct it. [3]
- The American Red Cross page classifies “chemical or object in eye,” blurred vision, loss of vision, bleeding in the eye, and continued eye pain among signs/symptoms requiring immediate emergency medical treatment in its first-aid material; it directs calling 9-1-1 and getting equipment when immediate emergency treatment is required. The extracted page did not provide a chemical-specific rinse duration or posture recommendation. [4]

These sources converge on avoiding delay, beginning irrigation with water, avoiding rubbing, and seeking urgent/emergency medical help. They do not by themselves establish that every chemical, exposure quantity, or symptom pattern has the same urgency, irrigation medium, duration, or destination of care.

## Population and jurisdiction limits

The NHS page is written for people using NHS services in the United Kingdom and uses A&E, 999, and NHS 111. [1] The NIOSH page is a United States occupational chemical-hazard reference and uses a general first-aid table rather than a patient-facing, chemical-specific assessment. [2] The Red Cross page uses US emergency terminology (9-1-1). [4] Mayo Clinic uses US emergency terminology but says “local emergency number” as an alternative. [3]

The pages are public general guidance, not evidence that a particular chemical is safe to manage outside emergency care. The cited sources do not provide a validated severity triage model by chemical identity, concentration, pH, amount, exposure duration, age, pregnancy, contact lenses, pre-existing eye disease, or ability to irrigate. They also do not establish that the same advice applies to a powder, aerosol, gas, contaminated contact lens, or simultaneous skin/airway exposure without additional hazard-specific assessment.

## Disagreements and uncertainties

- **Minimum rinse time:** NHS states at least 20 minutes for its general eye-washing instructions. [1] NIOSH states “large amounts of water” and immediate medical attention for chemical contact, while its separate frostbite entry specifies at least 15 minutes. [2] Mayo's extracted guidance emphasizes immediate medical help but does not state a single universal minimum rinse time in the relevant text. [3] These are not a basis for selecting one universal timer without clinician and toxicology review.
- **Water versus other fluids:** NHS and NIOSH specifically describe water. [1] [2] Mayo permits water or contact-lens saline rinse and warns against other substances and unsupervised eye drops. [3] The sources do not establish when saline is preferable, whether a particular commercial eyewash is suitable, or how product-specific instructions should interact with generic guidance.
- **Lid/contact-lens handling:** NIOSH mentions occasionally lifting upper and lower lids. [2] Mayo says to remove contact lenses if worn and not to rub. [3] The sources do not fully specify safe handling when a lens is stuck, the eyelids cannot be opened, or a penetrating injury is possible.
- **Emergency pathway:** “A&E,” “999,” “9-1-1,” ophthalmology, and “local emergency number” reflect different health systems and are not interchangeable product copy. [1] [3] [4] The sources do not validate a globally portable destination or call action.
- The Red Cross extraction identifies emergency signs but does not provide a chemical-specific rinse duration or posture recommendation. [4] This absence should not be interpreted as evidence that posture or duration is unimportant.

## What must not be inferred

- Do not infer that this note approves a LifeSafe chemical-eye-exposure protocol, diagnosis, triage algorithm, notification, or user-facing wording.
- Do not infer that “at least 15 minutes” or “at least 20 minutes” is a universal rule for every chemical or exposure. The cited time statements occur in different contexts and jurisdictions. [1] [2]
- Do not infer that rinsing replaces emergency assessment, poison-center/toxicology advice, ophthalmic assessment, or chemical-specific instructions.
- Do not infer that a product label, safety data sheet, or container is always available, legible, safe to retrieve, or appropriate to carry into an emergency setting.
- Do not infer that clear vision or improving symptoms rules out clinically important injury, delayed injury, or a need for assessment.
- Do not infer that the guidance applies unchanged to an embedded/penetrating object, thermal injury, gas exposure, powder exposure, skin exposure, ingestion, inhalation, or multiple casualties.
- Do not infer that US/UK emergency numbers, service names, or care destinations can be copied into other jurisdictions.
- Do not infer that the Red Cross signs/symptoms list is a complete exclusion or inclusion rule for emergency care. [4]

## Focused clinician review questions

1. For LifeSafe's intended countries, what chemical-eye-exposure definition and minimum emergency threshold are clinically defensible across acids, alkalis, solvents, detergents, powders, aerosols, and unknown substances?
2. Should the product direct immediate continuous irrigation before any questions, and if so, what water/saline wording, safe flow wording, contact-lens wording, and clinician-approved duration apply by exposure type?
3. What symptoms or mechanisms require emergency services versus urgent eye care versus poison-center/toxicology consultation, and how should those pathways vary by jurisdiction?
4. What should the product do when the person cannot open the eye, has severe pain or vision change, has a suspected penetrating injury, or has simultaneous airway/skin exposure?
5. What advice is safe for children, people with disabilities, contact-lens users, pregnancy, pre-existing eye disease, and situations where a second person is assisting?
6. Is it clinically and legally appropriate to suggest retaining or photographing the container/label, and what should be said if retrieving it would increase exposure risk?
7. What follow-up is needed after irrigation even when symptoms improve, and what delayed symptoms require re-escalation?
8. Which exact emergency-service names, numbers, poison-center routes, and translation/localization variants are approved for each launch jurisdiction?

## Language-review terms

These terms require clinician, safety, and localization review before any user-facing use: **chemical splash; chemical exposure; strong chemical; corrosive; acid; alkali/base; irritant; burn; chemical burn; rinse; flush; irrigate; clean water; saline; eyewash; hold the eye open; lift the eyelids; do not rub; contact lenses; remove lenses; embedded object; penetrating injury; vision changes; blurred vision; severe pain; light sensitivity/photophobia; emergency department; A&E; emergency services; ambulance; poison center/poison control; ophthalmologist; urgent eye care; local emergency number.** The review should also check whether “wash,” “rinse,” “flush,” and “irrigate” are understandable and safely translated in each target language, and whether “strong chemical” is too vague for the intended audience.
